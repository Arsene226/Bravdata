"How to ask patients for Google reviews" is one of the phrasings people type when they search this, along with "how to ask dental patients for reviews". We hold no volume tool and will not imply one, so we cannot say how often — but the question is real.

The short answer: ask at the end of the visit, in person, through a channel the patient already hears from you in, for their honest account, with nothing offered in exchange. Once, plus at most one reminder.

## When to ask

At the end of the appointment, while the visit is still what they are thinking about. The natural moment is when someone says thank you at the desk. Also fair: when someone rebooks, or when a problem you caused has just been put right.

Not fair: mid-treatment, when someone is in pain, or while they are still annoyed about a wait or a bill. Settle the complaint first — asking for a public review while someone is unhappy is a bad experience whatever they end up writing.

## Who to ask

Everyone you would want to hear from, the same way, every time — not only the patients you are confident are pleased. A steady habit is the only version that stays honest: choosing only the delighted makes the average say what you engineered rather than what happened.

## Which channel

Whichever one the patient already receives from you: a link they can tap in the message you send anyway, or a small card at the desk. One ask, one reminder. Rules about contacting patients differ by channel and change, so check the rule that applies to you with your own compliance advisor, not a blog post.

## What the ask must never say

Do not name the rating you want. "Please leave us a five-star review" asks someone to say something they may not mean, and turns an account into a favour. "How do you politely ask for a good review" is a real phrasing people search; the polite answer is that you ask for the review, not the stars.

Nothing in exchange either — no discount, no draw entry, no offer to remove a charge. Do not hand over a script for the patient to copy, and do not write reviews from the front desk. Bought or invented reviews break the platforms' own rules, and the risk lands under your name.

## Reply to what you get

Answer every review, briefly and factually. Thank them for specifics, and say what you changed if you changed something. Do not confirm that the person was ever a patient and do not describe anyone's care, because the reply is read by everyone who has not decided yet. More on that in [what not to do when marketing your San Antonio practice](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/what-not-to-do-marketing-your-san-antonio-practice).

## "How many do I need?"

That is a real phrasing too, and the honest answer is that we cannot name a count that produces a particular result — and we would distrust anyone who does. We hold no data on how review counts relate to where a practice appears, and a number from another market would not be evidence about yours.

The useful version is narrower: could someone deciding between you and another practice read enough recent, real accounts to feel they know what you are like? A habit answers that. A burst in one month does not.

## What this cannot tell you

- Whether reviews cause bookings.
- Whether a rating moves your position in anyone's results.
- What your competitors' reviews mean. We cannot see their patients or their books.
- Whether your way of asking fits the rules where you work.
