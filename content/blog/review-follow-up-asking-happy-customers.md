You know the moment. The job is finished, the customer looks at the work and says "that was faster than I expected." That is the minute to ask for a review — not three weeks later in a bulk email that lands when they have half forgotten your name.

What follows is the routine we would run: when to ask, who asks, what to say, how to make it one tap, what to leave out, and a weekly habit for what comes back. It is a judgement from practice, not a measured result — we hold no data on how many customers say yes.

## The moment that works

The point of happiness is short-lived: right after the work is done, while the customer is still looking at the result. In person if you are there — at the counter, on the driveway, at the door. In the message you send anyway if you are not, or in a short note the same day.

That beats a campaign weeks later: the customer can still remember what you did, and they can act in the same minute instead of filing it under "later". Whether it produces more reviews than a later email we cannot tell you — we have not run that test. Treat the timing as a judgement, and change it if your own experience says otherwise.

Not the moment: while the invoice is being argued over, or when something has gone wrong and is not fixed. Settle it first; an ask before the fix reads as pressure.

## Who should ask

The person the customer dealt with — the technician who did the work, the stylist, the person at the counter who booked the follow-up. Not a marketing department, and not an address the customer has never spoken to.

The ask only sounds like a favour between two people when it comes from the person who did the thing. "If you have a minute, would you mind saying that on our Google page?" is a sentence a technician can say on the way out; the same words from a brand account read like a request from a company.

## What to say

Short, specific, and in the customer's own language. Name the thing they were pleased about: it is what they would write anyway.

Generic: "Thanks for choosing us. Please leave us a review."

Specific: "Thanks for letting us sort the leak under the sink today. If you have a minute, would you mind saying how it went on our Google page? It helps the next person decide."

- **One ask per job.** Not three messages across two weeks.
- **Say what it is for, honestly.** "It helps people decide whether to call us" is true. "It helps us rank higher" is a claim you cannot check, and neither can we.
- **Give them the out, and mean it.** "No pressure at all" is not a trick line. Say it, then do not ask again this month.

## Make it one tap

A customer who has to search for your business to find the review box is a customer who stops. Remove the search.

- Get your review link from your Business Profile and test it on your own phone, logged out of your own account. If it is long, shorten it, then check the short version still opens the review box.
- Put a QR code where the customer already is: the counter, the van, the receipt, the card you hand over. Test it with a phone that is not yours.
- If you send a message anyway, put the link in that message rather than sending a second one.

Google's own guidance says you can "ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code", and that a customer "must be signed into a Google Account to leave a review" — an account can be made with a non-Gmail address. That is their wording, quoted from [their page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en), not our finding.

## What not to do

**Nothing in exchange.** No discount on the next visit, no draw entry, no free add-on, no refund in return for a review. Google's rule is not ambiguous, so here it is in their words: "Reviews and other user contributions to Google Maps must reflect a genuine experience. Offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews, change reviews, or remove negative reviews is considered fake & misleading content and is strictly prohibited." That is their rule quoted, dated the day we read it — not our finding, and not a claim we are adding to.

**No gating.** Gating is asking only the customers you expect to be positive and skipping the rest, so the page says what you selected for rather than what happened. It looks harmless and is not.

**Do not write it for them.** No drafting the review at the counter, no script handed over, no "here, I'll do it on your phone." A review written by the business is not a customer's account.

**No staff, family or bought lists.** Asking your own team, your relatives or a purchased list produces reviews from people who were never customers, and a bulk blast to a list you did not collect is a review problem and a consent problem at once.

## When the answer is lukewarm, or no

"Not really my thing" is a complete answer. Do not ask twice, do not make it awkward, and do not hear it as a verdict on the work.

If someone tells you something went wrong instead, take it offline: thank them, give a name and a number, and do not argue in a public thread. A public reply is read by people who have not decided yet, and a defensive one costs more than the review was worth.

One caution where being a customer is itself sensitive — healthcare, and services where being a client is private: do not confirm the relationship in public, and keep the detail out of any reply. The privacy-specific versions are already written: [answering a bad review without confirming a patient](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/answering-a-bad-review-without-confirming-a-patient) and [how to ask patients for Google reviews](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/how-to-ask-patients-for-google-reviews).

## A weekly habit

One short pass, same day each week, one named person:

- Check new reviews, and note the date you checked.
- Answer the ones with something to answer within a few business days — a real reply, not a template.
- Pass anything fixable to whoever can fix it. Three reviews mentioning the phone is a finding about the front desk, not three unlucky customers.
- Keep one line per review: the date, what they mentioned, what you did. In six months, that note is all you will have.

A routine, not a system: the smallest version that stays honest and survives a busy week.
