Most marketing advice you are handed as an independent practice owner is a list of things to buy. This is the other list — eight habits worth dropping, in the order we would flag them if we were reading your public presence for the first time.

Nothing below depends on a claim about the San Antonio market. We cannot see your competitors' bookings or their accounts, and we are not going to pretend we can. These habits are wrong in any city, which is also why they are worth fixing: not one of them requires a bigger budget.

One caveat before the list. None of this is legal, medical or regulatory advice. Where a rule applies to your practice — advertising, privacy, patient communication — check it with someone qualified rather than taking a blog post's word for it.

## Don't buy reviews, or write them yourself

A review is one person's account of what happened to them. Buying one, trading one with another business, or writing a few from the front desk changes whose account it is, and the platforms that host reviews treat it as a violation of their own rules — a risk you carry under your practice's name, not a shortcut. The legitimate version costs nothing: ask every patient you would want to hear from, the same way every time, with nothing offered in exchange, and take the answer whether or not you like it.

## Don't pay for a promise about where you will appear in search

No vendor controls the order of search results. Those results come from systems that change without notice, so anyone who names a position for you is either describing a coincidence or saying something they cannot support. Ask for one sentence in writing: that they do not promise a search position, a ranking, a number of patients or a revenue figure. The rest of the questions worth asking are in [questions to ask before you sign a marketing contract](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/questions-to-ask-before-you-sign-a-marketing-contract). It applies to your own copy too: if a page or an ad compares you with other practices, it should rest on a fact you can point to, and ratings move.

## Don't set your business listing up once and never open it again

For many local practices the listing is where the decision happens — someone taps the map result, checks the hours, taps the number. It drifts because it is edited from three places: the website somebody updates yearly, the listing nobody has opened since setup, and the booking tool with its own settings page. Summer hours in one, a second phone number in another, a holiday closure in a third. Fixing a contradiction usually takes minutes and needs no new brand. [Check your hours, phone and services first](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/hours-phone-and-services-first) is the short version of that pass.

## Don't publish patient photos or testimonials without written permission

A before-and-after photograph, a patient smiling in the chair, a screenshot of a review with a name on it — these say something about a real person, and a verbal yes on a busy afternoon is not the same as permission. Ask in writing, say where the picture will appear and for how long, and keep the note. Health-privacy rules, your profession's advertising rules and each platform's own policies may all apply, and they differ by state and by setting, so check the ones that apply where you work. Being a patient is agreement to be treated, not agreement to be marketed.

## Don't argue with a negative review in public

A reply is read by everyone who has not decided yet, not only by the person who wrote it. Answer once, briefly and factually, without confirming that the person was ever a patient and without describing anyone's care — that last part is where a bad afternoon becomes a real problem. If you changed something as a result, say what you changed. Do not offer anything in exchange for the review coming down. And if the complaint is about a bill or a wait, those are things you can fix without naming anybody.

## Don't turn your appointment list into a marketing list

A booking, a reminder text and a portal message are each permission for that message. None of them is permission to send promotions, and holding someone's number is not the same as holding their agreement. Keep the lists apart, make opting out easy, and honour it the first time — including when the message is one you are pleased with. The rules on contacting patients differ by channel and change over time, so check the current ones.

## Don't publish filler to keep a calendar looking full

A posting schedule is a tool, not an instruction. If nothing has happened at the practice worth telling a customer about, a made-up wellness tip or a stock photo with a quote over it does not fill the gap; it spends the attention you were saving for the week something real happened. Quiet is honest and costs nothing. And do not let an automation write in a voice your practice would never use on the phone — a post that sounds like a different business is read as one.

## Don't judge the work by numbers that can only go up

Impressions, reach and followers can climb while the phone stays quiet. They are not wrong to look at, but none of them is a patient, and a figure with no source and no date cannot be checked by anybody. Ask for the number, where it came from, the period it covers, and what was actually done that month. [What 'not measurable yet' means](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/what-not-measurable-yet-means) is why a report that cannot print a blank is a report you cannot audit.

## What to do instead

The short version: get the listing right, ask for reviews and take the answers, write down what you promise and what you do not, and get permission before you publish anything about a person. None of that needs a retainer.

## What this list cannot tell you

- Whether any of these is costing you customers. Nobody can read that off a listing, including us.
- Whether your competitors are doing them. We cannot see their accounts, their budgets or their bookings.
- Which rules apply to your practice. That depends on your state, your profession and the platform — ask someone qualified.
- Whether a vendor is good in other respects.
