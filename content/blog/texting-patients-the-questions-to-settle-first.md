"Is texting patients HIPAA compliant", "is texting patients a HIPAA violation", "HIPAA texting rules" — real phrasings people type when they search this. We hold no volume tool, so we cannot say how often.

What follows does not answer any of them. This article is not legal advice and does not tell you whether texting patients is allowed for your practice; no blog post honestly can. Whether your arrangement fits the rule that applies to you is a judgment for your own compliance advisor, with your practice's facts in front of them. What this page does is lay out the questions to settle, in order, so that conversation starts somewhere useful.

## 1. What is going in the message?

A policy that treats the three kinds as one will not survive being read line by line. Separate them:

- **Logistics** — a reminder, a time, a place, a parking note.
- **Care** — a result, an instruction, anything clinical.
- **Marketing** — a new service, an offer, a request for a review.

Decide which of the three you intend to send before you choose a tool; everything below depends on it.

## 2. What did each patient agree to, in what words?

Write down the sentence a patient agreed to, the date, and which of the three kinds of message it covers. Keep it where you can find it in a year, because the question that arrives later is always "what did they agree to, and when."

Our own rule in the product is that connecting an account is not marketing consent, and the same distinction belongs here: permission to reach someone is not permission to promote to them. A patient who texts you to book, or hands over a number for reminders, has not agreed to receive offers. Treat those as three separate permissions until your advisor tells you otherwise.

## 3. What does the vendor do with the content?

Ask, in writing: where messages are stored, for how long, who can read them, whether they are used for anything else, what happens when you leave, and whether the vendor will sign what your advisor asks for. A one-word assurance in a chat window is not the record you need.

## 4. Who answers, and how fast?

A patient replies to an automated reminder with "I'm in pain." Who reads that, within what time, and what are they told to do? Does the number accept replies? Is anyone minding it on a Sunday evening? If the answer is nobody, the message should say what the number is for and where to go instead.

## 5. How does someone stop, and who handles a wrong number?

Someone replies "stop", or changes their number so the next reminder reaches a stranger. Decide who processes that, how quickly, and how you would know. A suppression list that nobody owns is not a suppression list.

## What good looks like afterwards

One page, not a policy library. What you send, to whom, under which agreement, through which vendor, answered by whom, stopped how, reviewed when. If you cannot fit it on a page, you have not finished deciding.

## What this cannot tell you

- Whether your arrangement fits the rules that apply to you. Ask your own compliance advisor; we are a marketing team, not one.
- Whether a vendor's claims about itself are true. Ask for it in writing and let your advisor read it.
- What your patients would rather receive. Ask them directly.
- Whether texting reduces no-shows or increases bookings. We hold no data on that and will not invent one.
