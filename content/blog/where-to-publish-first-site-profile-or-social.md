An owner sits down with an hour and something worth saying: a new service, a change of hours, a piece of advice a customer asked for twice this week. Where does it go first?

Our answer, decided and written down rather than improvised: **your own website first, your Google Business Profile second, social last.** Below is the reasoning, so you can check it against your own situation rather than take it on faith — and, for each of the three, what it can and cannot do for a local business.

## The reasoning, in one line each

- **Your website is the only one of the three you own.** You can edit it, move it, keep the page when a platform changes its mind, and point to it from anywhere.
- **Your Google Business Profile is where a customer confirms the details.** It is the panel that answers a search for your name or your service plus your town, and you control only part of it.
- **Social is where people check that you are real and current.** It is the last stop in the sequence, not because it is worthless, but because it is rented space that sinks out of sight fastest.

None of the three is a shortcut past the others. A post does not fix a website that never says what you do, and a website does not fix a listing with the wrong hours.

## First: your own site — and WordPress first of all

If you have the choice of platform, publish on your own site before anywhere else, and if you are choosing a platform for it, choose WordPress before a closed page builder.

The reason is not taste. It is that with WordPress you own the content and the database: the text you write lives in a place you can export, back up and move, and no single company can decide one morning that your page no longer exists. That matters for the same reason a signed lease matters — you are building something you may want to keep for years. A hosted builder can be perfectly good, but check that you can take the words with you if you ever leave.

Practically, a service page on your own site is the only place where you can:

- **say the whole thing**, in your own words, with as much detail as the subject deserves;
- **name the area you genuinely serve**, and the services you actually offer;
- **put the next step on the page** — call, book, directions, a form — instead of hoping someone taps through a profile;
- **keep the page**, and update it next year, without it disappearing into a feed.

**What your own site cannot do.** It does not get discovered by itself. A page nobody links to and nobody searches for is a page nobody reads, and no amount of writing guarantees a position in anyone's results. It also cannot collect reviews for you, and it cannot stop a customer from choosing the business that answered the phone first.

If you are unsure whether your site needs a rebuild or just a few fixes, that is its own decision: see [do you need a new website, or fixes?](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/new-website-or-fixes-a-decision-list).

## Second: your Google Business Profile

Once the page exists, put the plain facts where people look first. The sequence a customer often follows is: see you in a map or a search result, check the hours and the phone number, then decide whether to look at your site at all.

Your profile is where those facts belong, and it is the only one of the three where the platform assembles part of the answer from public information — which is why it can be right about your name and wrong about your Saturday hours.

What it can do:

- **answer "are they open, are they near me, what is the number"** in the moment someone is deciding;
- **carry photographs of the real place**, which help a nervous first-time customer know what they are walking into;
- **hold reviews and your replies to them**, in public, attached to your name;
- **show a post for a limited time**, which is useful for a closure or a change of hours.

**What it cannot do.** You do not own it. You control the fields, not the display, not the ranking, and not which competitors appear beside you. A profile cannot promise you a position in the local results, and Google's own guidance describes local results as a matter of relevance, distance and prominence rather than something a business can buy its way into. Posts on a profile are not pages: they are temporary, they do not accumulate into a body of work, and they do not replace a page on your own site.

The full walk-through of what you can edit is in [your Google Business Profile: the owner's list](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/your-google-business-profile-the-owners-list).

## Third: social

Social comes last, and that is a decision about durability, not about importance. A social account is the fastest of the three to update and the shortest-lived: a post is visible to a slice of the people who already follow you, for a while, and then it is effectively gone. Nothing you post there becomes a page a stranger finds two years from now when they search for what you do.

What social can do:

- **show the human side** — the people, the place, the way you work;
- **reach people who already follow you**, including customers who would happily recommend you;
- **give an owner a low-effort way to stay visibly current**, which is worth something on its own.

**What it cannot do.** You do not own the account or the audience. Reach is decided by the platform and can change without notice. A post is not a permission slip for anything else, and it is not a substitute for a page that explains your services or a profile with correct hours. It also cannot be counted on to bring new customers in a way you can measure — and anyone who tells you a post count produces a patient or customer count is guessing.

For a practice, the honest split is usually: the service page explains the procedure, the profile carries the hours and the number, and social shows that a person answers. That last part is real, but it is not the foundation.

## If you only have one hour

1. **Ten minutes: fix the disagreements.** Put your website, your profile and your booking page side by side. Different hours, a number that rings nowhere, a service only one of them lists. Fix those first — they are the cheapest thing on any list.
2. **Thirty minutes: write the page you keep needing.** One service, in the words customers use, on your own site. Publish it, then link to it from the profile where the platform allows a link.
3. **Twenty minutes: one profile update.** Confirm hours, phone, category and services. Add a real photograph of the place if the last one is old.
4. **Then, if there is time left, post it to social** — and point back to the page, so the work does not disappear with the post.

That order is the whole argument: the hour goes first into the thing you own, then into the thing people check, then into the thing that fades.

## What this sequence cannot tell you

- **Your position in anyone's results.** Nothing here promises a ranking, a local-pack appearance or a number of views.
- **How many customers any of it produces.** We are not going to attach a number to a page or a post that we cannot show.
- **That one channel caused anything.** If a quiet month follows a busy one, that is a comparison, not a cause.
- **Whether social or search matters more for your business.** That depends on what you sell and how your customers look for it, and we have no data on yours.
