"How do I respond to a bad review on Google" and "how to respond to negative Google reviews" are the phrasings people type when this happens. We hold no volume tool, so we cannot say how often — but the question is real, and it arrives on a bad morning.

Two things are true before any wording: a reply is public, and a reply is not a place to confirm that someone was a patient. This is not legal advice and not compliance guidance — whether a sentence is safe for your practice is a question for your compliance advisor or privacy officer, with your facts in front of them. What follows is a structure, not a ruling on what you may publish.

## A reply is public, and everyone else reads it

The person who wrote the review may never see your answer. The people deciding between you and another practice will. That is why a reply written as a rebuttal reads badly even when the practice was right: it becomes public record under your practice's name, and it stays after the afternoon that prompted it is forgotten.

## The structure: acknowledge, don't diagnose, don't confirm, move it offline

Four moves, in order, one reply.

**Acknowledge what you can check.** Thank them for telling you, and if it is true, name what you can verify — a wait, a closure, a phone system. Do not accept what you cannot verify; do not argue with it either.

**Don't diagnose.** No clinical detail of any kind: not the treatment, not the outcome, not "that is a normal reaction."

**Don't confirm who they are.** This is the part that gets skipped, and it is the whole article.

**Move it offline, with a route that works.** "Please call the office and ask for the practice manager during business hours" — a real number, answered by a real person. Say once that you want to put it right, then stop.

## Where a reply confirms a patient by accident

Each of these states or hints that you know who they are as a patient — avoid rather than soften:

- "Thank you for being a patient with us."
- "We are sorry your visit on the 14th did not go as planned."
- "As we discussed at your appointment."
- "We have reviewed your records."
- Using the reviewer's name when the review is anonymous.
- Answering a review that describes a treatment as though you recognise the case.

None is needed to answer well.

## Removal is the platform's decision, not yours

A review can be reported for breaking the platform's policies — a threat, someone else's personal information, someone who was never a patient. It may be removed. It may not. We will not promise a removal, and neither should anyone selling you reputation work. You can ask, and keep the reply civil meanwhile. Do not offer anything in exchange for it coming down.

## Write it once, and watch for the pattern

If three recent reviews mention the phone, treat that as a finding about the front desk rather than three unlucky patients. Fix the thing, then answer. ## What this cannot tell you

- Whether a specific reply is safe for your practice. Ask your own compliance advisor; we are a marketing team.
- Whether a review will be removed.
- Whether replying changes a rating or where you appear in search. We hold no data on either.
- Whether the complaint was fair. You can read the review; you were not in the room, and neither were we.
