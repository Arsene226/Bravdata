"HIPAA marketing authorization", "HIPAA marketing consent", "HIPAA marketing rules" — the phrasings people type when working out what a practice may put in front of the public. We hold no volume tool and will not imply one, so we cannot say how often.

This article does not answer them. It is not legal advice and not compliance guidance, and we are not your compliance advisor or privacy officer. No blog post can tell you what your practice may post. What this page does is take the general search apart into the pieces your advisor will need.

## Why "consent" and "authorization" keep coming up

Both words turn up in the same search, and practice owners notice, because they seem to be doing different jobs. Whether they do — for your message, in your state, for your kind of practice — is what this article will not decide.

What we can say is why settling it first is worth the delay: whichever word applies, the conversation later is much shorter with a written record of what someone agreed to, in what words, on what date, covering what. A caption published on a guess is awkward to unwind; the record is easy if you decided first.

## 1. What exactly are you promoting?

A new service, a new location, a new clinician, a flu-shot reminder, a review request, a photo of the team. Write the sentence you intend to publish before anything else. "Marketing" as a category is too broad to put to anyone; the sentence is not.

## 2. Who is it going to?

Everyone who follows the page, a list of patients, one person, or the public at large with a boosted post. That answer changes which questions come next, and owners often skip it: the caption gets written before anyone asks who will read it.

## 3. Which list did that name come from?

If patients are involved, name the system: the practice-management software, the appointment book, a spreadsheet built for one promotion. Then ask whether being on that list is the same as having agreed to hear this. Our own rule — connecting an account is not marketing consent — is a smaller version of it.

## 4. What permission do you actually hold, in what words?

Not "they are a patient of ours." The sentence, where it was captured, and when. If nothing was written down, that is a finding rather than a failure: it tells you what to fix before the next send, not after.

## 5. Which channel, and who answers?

A post on your own page, an email, a text, a card at the desk. Each reaches people differently and carries its own rules, which differ by channel and change — check the current ones with your advisor. Then decide who reads the replies, and how fast. [Texting patients: the questions to settle first](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/texting-patients-the-questions-to-settle-first) takes that part further.

## What to do with the answers

One page: what you are promoting, to whom, from which list, under which written permission, on which channel, answered by whom. Hand it to your advisor with your practice's facts in front of them and ask which line they would change.

## What this cannot tell you

- Whether your arrangement fits the rule that applies to you. Ask your own compliance advisor; we are a marketing team.
- Which form, agreement or notice your practice needs. We name none.
- What your patients would rather receive. Ask them directly.
- Whether any of this changes where you appear in search. It does not follow, and we hold no data on it.
