If you rank when someone searches your business name but not when they search for what you sell, you are looking at a frustratingly common gap — and the first thing to fix is usually not a new website or a marketing tool.

Local SEO is not a trick. It is whether a stranger searching for what you do, near you, can find you and act on it in one tap. If you own a small business and also answer the phone, here are the four places to start, in order.

## Local SEO for small business: what it means when you're the one doing it

Local SEO means making it clear to search engines and potential customers what your business does, where it serves people, and how to get in touch. It includes the information on your business listing and website, as well as the experience after someone finds you. You do not need to master marketing jargon to begin; you need accurate details and clear answers to customer questions.

## The four things that decide it

### 1. Get your Google Business Profile details right

Start with the plain fields: business name, primary category, hours, phone number and services. Use the name customers know you by, choose a category that accurately describes your main work, and list only services you actually provide. Check that your hours and phone match what appears on your website and booking page.

Google's own [Business Profile guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) recommends keeping business information complete and up to date. It also explains that local results are influenced by relevance, distance and prominence, not by a paid shortcut. Accurate information helps customers and search engines understand your business, but it cannot guarantee a particular position.

**Check this today:** Open your profile and website on your phone. Confirm the hours, phone number and main services match. For a fuller walk-through, see [your Google Business Profile: the owner's list](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/your-google-business-profile-the-owners-list).

### 2. Put the customer's words on your own pages

A listing can tell people what you do, but your website should explain it clearly too. Give each important service its own page where possible, describe it in the terms customers use, and name the town or area you genuinely serve. A customer looking for "boiler repair in Oak Park," for example, should not have to guess whether your site covers boiler repairs or that area.

This does not mean repeating a phrase until it sounds unnatural. It means using plain language that matches the question a customer would actually ask. If customers find you by name but not by service, the words may simply be missing from the pages you control. Here's a practical explanation of [why customers find you by name only](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/why-customers-find-you-by-name-only).

**Check this today:** Write down three phrases customers have used when asking about your services. Look for those phrases, or clear equivalents, on your website. If there is no relevant page, start with the service you most want people to find.

### 3. Ask for genuine reviews and reply to them

Reviews help potential customers judge whether your business might be right for them. A steady, straightforward habit is more useful than a one-off push: ask customers after a real experience, and reply in a professional, human way. You do not need to offer a discount or gift for a review; Google's [review guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en) says incentives in exchange for reviews are prohibited.

When replying, keep it brief and specific. Thank people for useful feedback, address concerns without arguing, and take sensitive conversations offline when appropriate. For medical practices and other privacy-sensitive businesses, avoid confirming that someone is a patient or customer: [answering a bad review without confirming a patient](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/answering-a-bad-review-without-confirming-a-patient) covers the wording, and [how to ask patients for Google reviews](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/how-to-ask-patients-for-google-reviews) covers the ask itself. If you want the general routine for a business of any kind — when to ask, who asks, and what to say — it is here: [review follow-up: asking happy customers for a Google review](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/review-follow-up-asking-happy-customers).

**Check this today:** Look at your latest reviews. Reply to any that need a response, then decide on a simple, appropriate moment to ask future customers for honest feedback.

### 4. Make the next step work after the click

Being found is only part of the job. If a customer taps your number and reaches a dead line, sees confusing booking instructions, or calls while you are unavailable with no clear callback option, the path from search to service has a gap.

Make it easy to understand what to do next: call, book, request an estimate, or visit. Put the right option somewhere easy to spot, and make sure the phone number can be tapped on a mobile screen. Then check that somebody is responsible for responding.

**Check this today:** Pretend you are a new customer. Tap your phone number, try the booking link and read the hours. Does each step work, and does the next step make sense? If details are inconsistent, start with [hours, phone and services first](https://www.bravdata.io/blog/hours-phone-and-services-first).

## What to skip for now

- **Do not buy a link package.** A pile of paid links is not a substitute for accurate business details and useful pages, and it can be difficult to judge the quality of what you are buying.
- **Do not stuff every nearby town name into a page.** It reads poorly and can mislead customers about where you actually work.
- **Do not rebuild your website before fixing the fields.** A redesign will not correct wrong hours, a broken phone link or missing service information by itself.
- **Do not pay for a tool you will open once.** Start with the free information you already control; add a tool only when you know what question it needs to answer.

## What local SEO cannot tell you

No honest guide can promise a specific position. Search results vary by person, place and day, and Google says that distance is one of the factors in local results. You also cannot promise a certain number of customers or a particular amount of revenue from these changes. And a competitor's public listing does not reveal their private numbers or tell you exactly why they appeared for a search.
